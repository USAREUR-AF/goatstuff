THE BIGGER PICTURE

What zero trust really is, in plain language.

◀ TO THE PLAYGROUND 📖 GLOSSARY

There is no inside.
Nothing is trusted by location — only by proof, every time.

"Zero trust" usually lands as a slogan, and most people picture it wrong: that there's a wall, and once you're past it you're in — and that the point is catching attackers. Both miss it. There is no inside; every action is re-checked by several complementary aspects of identity and access. And most of what they handle — a noisy service, a stale credential, a thing that needs replacing — was never an attack at all.

THE OLD WAY

One wall, then free run

A castle with a moat: you're checked once at the gate, and after that you roam the inside freely. It feels safe — but anything that gets in (a stranger who tailgates, a service gone haywire, a credential no one re-checks) can move sideways to everything. The wall is the only line, and there's no second one.

ZERO TRUST

There is no inside

Now there are no hallways at all — only sealed rooms, each reached by its own single-use link that re-proves identity and then vanishes. A breach floods one room and stops; there's nowhere to move sideways. Credentials are short-lived, so a stolen one dies on its own. Like a submarine's watertight compartments — one floods, the ship stays afloat.

THE REAL JOB

It manages every access risk — not just attackers

Good identity and access management isn't a villain-detector. It mediates the whole range of things that go wrong around access: a service flooding the channel, a credential gone stale, something mid-replacement, an honest misconfiguration — and a bad actor is just one more case the same machinery handles. Get the everyday cases right and the hostile one is mostly already covered.

And because every action is bound to an identity, when something does need cutting off there's more than one place to do it — at the layer that owns the actor. Pull its mesh access and it can't reach anything at all (immediate, and the lever we actually use); strip its write permission in the fabric's policy (a coarser, slower cut); and the credential itself isn't really "revoked" — it's built to expire on its own (fast for short-lived workload identities, slower for issued certs). Different layers, different speeds, one identity — defense in depth, not a single kill-switch.

THE HUMAN TRUTH

You can't delete human error — so limit the blast

The overwhelming majority of real-world breaches — commonly put near 99% — start with people: someone gets phished, or makes a plain configuration blunder (a weak password, a box left open to the internet). Because the root cause is human, it can never be fully eliminated — only reduced, watched for, and managed. So real security isn't a taller wall. It reduces the chances these happen, watches for them, reacts fast — and, above all, limits the blast radius when one slips through. Same incident, two worlds — watch where it stops:

EVERY BYTE, TWICE

Encrypted end-to-end — twice over

Identity decides who may act; encryption keeps the traffic private and untampered on the way. Every interaction on the fabric rides inside two independent encrypted tunnels, one wrapped inside the other — so reading the wire reveals nothing, and breaking one layer still leaves the other intact.

One tunnel inside the other

How a connection comes up

① The outer tunnel — WireGuard

The mesh itself (NetBird on WireGuard) encrypts everything between two peers at the network layer. Anything on the wire is already ciphertext.

② The inner tunnel — mTLS

Inside that, the application opens its own mutual-TLS connection (here, in Zenoh). It encrypts and proves both identities at once. Other applications bring their own TLS — usually mutual too.

Swappable by design

The two layers differ in origin and algorithm, and either can change without the other — including a move to post-quantum ciphers as they mature. No single algorithm is load-bearing.

And at rest

Beyond the wire: disks are encrypted at rest (full-disk LUKS), keys are short-lived with forward secrecy (a stolen key can't decrypt past traffic), and the crypto can run in FIPS-validated mode where required.

THE PAYOFF

The freedom it buys

The same machinery that contains misbehavior is what makes identity and access fast and local to manage — where the old way was slow and centralized.

Sovereign & federated

A group creates, changes, and revokes its own identities on its own authority — no funneling every change through one central gatekeeper.

Dynamic

Granting, scoping, and rescinding access is a live change, not a rebuild: bring a participant online now, take them offline cleanly later, narrow what a service can touch the moment its role changes.

Self-healing

Because identity is re-verified per connection and credentials expire on their own, much "cleanup" needs no human: stale things lapse, replaced things re-prove. And a flooding producer is tied to its identity — it shows up on the health surface and its credential can be revoked to cut it off (automatic rate-throttle is on the roadmap).

Disposable services

A name only says where, never whether to trust — so you can tear a service down and stand a fresh one up under the same name in seconds, and everyone keeps working. (Try it in the playground.)

THE FINE PRINT

What's actually running

The playground runs the real components — a real CA, real SPIRE, real OIDC, a real access decision — so what you see is the system actually working, not a mock. The one exception is network admission (NetBird): a browser can't perform a WireGuard handshake, so that layer is represented rather than live, and labeled as such.
